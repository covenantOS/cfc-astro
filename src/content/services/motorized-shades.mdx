## When Motorizing Is Worth It, and When It Is Not

Custom motorized shades generally run **$450 to $1,500 per window installed**, which is where most of the market sits nationally. A battery roller on a standard bedroom window lands near the bottom; a wide slider, a hardwired motor, or a designer fabric moves toward the top. Motorizing adds a few hundred dollars per opening over the same shade with a manual lift, so the honest question is which windows deserve it. Daily-use windows are the clearest case: the slider to the patio, the bedroom shades you run morning and night, the wall of glass facing the afternoon sun. Unreachable windows are the other case, and there the motor is not a convenience at all but the only practical way to operate the shade. Large or heavy shades belong on the list too, because a wide roller with a manual chain gets awkward fast.

The windows that rarely justify it are the ones nobody touches. A guest room shade you set once a season does not need a motor, and putting one there is how a quote gets expensive without making the house better to live in. We will tell you which windows in your own home fall into which group.

## The Motorized Shades We Install in St. Petersburg

Almost any shade we make can be motorized, and the fabric decision comes before the motor decision:

- **Motorized roller shades.** The most common choice, and the cleanest look. A single fabric panel on a tube, in solar, light-filtering, or room-darkening weaves.
- **Motorized blackout shades.** Room-darkening fabric with side channels where you want true darkness, popular in bedrooms and media rooms.
- **Motorized solar shades.** Open-weave fabric rated by openness factor, so you keep the daytime view out while cutting heat and glare. The workhorse on west-facing glass in Florida.
- **Motorized cellular and Roman shades.** Where you want the insulating structure of a cellular shade or the soft fold of a Roman, with the lift automated.
- **Motorized patio and lanai shades.** Exterior-rated fabric and hardware for wide openings, covered in more depth on our [outdoor window shades](/services/outdoor-window-shades/) page.

## Battery or Hardwired: The Decision That Actually Matters

Most homeowners ask about brands first, and the more useful question is power. Rechargeable battery motors need no wiring, which is why they are the default in an existing home. You charge them once or twice a year with a cable, the same way you charge a cordless vacuum, and no one opens a wall. The tradeoff is that recharging is a chore you have to remember, and on a shade that cycles several times a day the interval gets shorter.

Hardwired motors never need charging and are the better answer in new construction, in a remodel where the drywall is already open, or on very large shades that run constantly. The cost is the wiring itself, which is real if the wall is closed. There is a middle path we use often: hardwire the handful of shades that run all day, and leave the rest on battery. On the hardware side we work from the same [brand libraries](/brands/) we use for the rest of the house, so the motor and the fabric are specified together rather than bolted on afterward.

## Smart Home Control, Scheduling, and Florida Sun

Remote control is the entry point, and scheduling is what changes how the house feels. Most systems we install connect to a hub that works with Alexa, Google Assistant, and Apple HomeKit, and larger integrated systems tie into whole-home automation alongside lighting. That means a single scene can drop the west shades, dim the lights, and close the drapery at once.

The scheduling piece matters more in St. Pete than it does in most of the country. Set the west-facing shades to lower at two in the afternoon and the room stays usable through the worst of the day, the air conditioning cycles less, and the sun stops bleaching your floors and upholstery. Some systems run off a sun sensor instead of a clock, so a cloudy day does not get the same treatment as a bright one. It is the same reasoning behind exterior [window shades](/services/window-shades/) and [plantation shutters](/services/plantation-shutters/), applied on a timer.

## Measuring, Mounting, and Why Installation Decides the Result

A motorized shade is a mechanical thing running on a track, so the tolerances are tighter than they are on a manual shade. The shade has to be square to the opening or the fabric tracks to one side over time. The mounting has to be solid, particularly on the concrete block and stucco common in older St. Pete construction, because a motor puts load on the bracket every cycle. Wide shades need the tube and motor sized to the span, otherwise the fabric deflects in the middle.

None of that is exotic, and all of it is why we measure and install ourselves rather than handing you a box. The same install crew that hangs our [custom blinds](/services/custom-blinds/) and drapery does the motorized work, and they set the limits, pair the remotes, and confirm every shade runs cleanly before they leave.

## Motorized Shades vs. Off-the-Shelf Smart Blinds

Big-box smart blinds are cheaper up front and they work, within limits. They come in stock widths, which means a gap at the jamb on a window that is not a standard size, and light leaks through that gap on exactly the bedroom shade you bought for darkness. The fabric range is narrow, the motors are what they are, and installation is your problem, including the mounting question above.

A made-to-measure motorized shade is cut to your opening, in a fabric picked for that room's light and exposure, on a motor sized to the shade, installed and tuned by the people who measured it. On a small window in a spare room, the off-the-shelf version is a reasonable call. On a wall of glass in the room where you spend your evenings, the difference shows every day.
